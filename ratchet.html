11 Bench Shop / engineering: ratchet and pawl (one-way motion)

One way only.

Fine tooth takes more clicks to come around. Coarse tooth takes fewer, larger steps. With the pawl engaged, clockwise drive is allowed and reverse is blocked. Lift the pawl and the wheel can turn either way.

What a ratchet is for

Drive, then hold

A ratchet and pawl gives one-way motion. The drive turns the wheel clockwise. The pawl sits in a tooth and stops the wheel from turning back.

The wheel has 36 teeth, so one turn is 36 clicks and each click is 10°.

The pawl sits in a tooth. Clockwise drive is allowed, and the pawl rides up the tooth and drops into the next one. That drop is the click. Reverse is blocked, so the wheel cannot give back the step it just took.

This bench does not give the pawl a spring, and it does not ask how strong a tooth is. The pawl is either in the teeth, holding, or lifted clear so the wheel freewheels.

Try this

Select Fine tooth with the pawl engaged. Count is 36 and the step is 10°. Drive one step and watch the pawl drop. Then try reverse. The wheel should stop, and the readout should say the pawl is holding. Select Coarse tooth. Count is 8 and the step is 45°. One click moves much farther. Lift the pawl, drive, then reverse. The wheel turns both ways.

11.1

Learning objectives

  • State that a pawl in the teeth allows drive in one direction and blocks the reverse.
  • Compute the step angle as 360° divided by the tooth count.
  • Explain that a click is the pawl dropping into the next tooth.
  • Explain that lifting the pawl lets the wheel freewheel in either direction.

11.2

Classroom loop

  1. Cover the readout. Set Fine tooth, pawl engaged. Students say how many clicks make one full turn, and what 360° ÷ 36 is.
  2. Uncover 36 teeth and 10°. Drive one step and name the click as the pawl drops.
  3. Try reverse. The wheel stops. Students should say the pawl is holding.
  4. Select Coarse tooth and compare 45° with 10°. Then lift the pawl and reverse the wheel. It freewheels.

11.3

On the job

A mechanic using a ratchet wrench and a socket set pulls the handle to turn a fastener, then swings the handle back for the next pull. The pawl holds the socket on the return, so the fastener does not turn loose and the mechanic does not lose the turn already taken. That is what the pawl is for: you can pull in the drive direction without giving the position back. Flip the lever and the same pawl holds the other way, which is how the wrench loosens.

A millwright on a hoist or a winch uses a holding pawl on the drum. The drive can lift the load. When the lift stops, the pawl drops into a tooth and keeps the drum from running backward, so the load stays at the height the crew just gained. Without that pawl the drum would pay out the moment the drive let go. The pawl lets the crew lift in one direction without losing position. Lifting the pawl on purpose, with the brake set, is how the drum is allowed to lower.

A bicycle technician sees the same one-way hold in a freehub or a freewheel. Pedaling drives the pawls into the teeth and turns the wheel. Coasting lets the wheel run ahead while the pawls click over the teeth, so the pedals are not forced around. The moment the rider pedals again, the pawls catch, and the wheel is still where the ride left it. In each of these jobs the pawl lets you pull or lift in one direction without losing the position you just gained.
