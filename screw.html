07 Bench Shop / engineering: screw (helical incline — pitch vs effort)

Change the pitch.

The pitch slider sits with the readout. It is the rise of one turn. Mean diameter sets the circumference the effort walks. The clamp does not change.

What a screw is

A ramp, wound up

A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Unwrap one turn and you get a right triangle. The base is the mean circumference of the thread. The height is the pitch.

Mean circumference divided by pitch is the ideal mechanical advantage. Effort is the 10 lb clamp divided by that advantage.

A finer pitch is a shallower ramp. The same clamp takes less tangential effort, and the jaw advances less each turn.

The effort arrow is tangent to the thread, at the mean circumference. A long vise handle is a second lever on top of this screw. This model leaves that handle out, and it leaves friction out.

Try this

Start on Fine, pitch 0.100 in. Read the effort. Switch to Vise, pitch 0.200 in. The pitch doubled, so the advantage is cut in half and the effort doubles. Press One turn on each and compare how far the jaw travels.

Pitch versuseffort.

Use this page next to a vise screw or a C-clamp. The classroom use is the helical incline: pitch versus effort for a constant 10 lb clamp.

07.1

Learning objectives

  • Say that a screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder.
  • Compute ideal mechanical advantage as mean circumference ÷ pitch.
  • Predict the tangential effort for a 10 lb clamp, then check the readout.
  • State the tradeoff: a finer pitch takes less effort and advances less per turn.

07.2

Classroom loop

  1. Set Vise. Cover the readout. Mean diameter is 1.00 in and pitch is 0.200 in. Students compute π × 1.00, then divide by 0.200.
  2. Uncover the advantage and the effort. The effort should be about 0.64 lb.
  3. Switch to Fine, then to Coarse. Effort falls, then rises. Jaw travel per turn does the same.
  4. Name what a real vise adds: handle length, and friction that makes it harder to turn.

07.3

On the job

A screw shows up wherever a turn has to become a strong, slow push. A mechanic winds a bottle jack under a corner of a car. A millwright sets a machine on screw jacks and turns them until the base sits level. A bottle cap is the same machine in the hand: a fine thread, so a small twist pulls the lid down hard against the seal.

Pitch is why those jobs feel different. A fine thread lifts a heavy load with a light pull, and the load rises only a little each turn. A coarse thread closes faster and asks for more effort. Knowing that tells you how the handle should feel before the clamp is tight, and it keeps a jack from climbing faster than you meant.
