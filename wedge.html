08 Bench Shop / engineering: wedge (splitting force — length ÷ thickness)

Change the wedge.

The split stays 10 lb. Move the slope length or the thickness beside the wedge. Ideal mechanical advantage is slope length divided by thickness.

What a wedge is for

Less force, longer drive

A wedge is two inclined planes set back to back. One face can also sit against a stop. Ideal mechanical advantage is the slope length along the face divided by the thickness. Thickness is the width of the thick end.

The wedge is long and thin, so the push is a small share of the split. You drive it a long way to open a narrow gap.

You push 1.25 lb along 4.00 in. The split opens 0.50 in. Ideal work matches either way: 5.0 in·lb.

The load stays 10 lb. This model leaves out friction. On a real wedge, friction makes the push harder.

Try this

Select Chisel. Cover the readout. Slope length is 4.00 in and thickness is 0.50 in. Compute 4 ÷ 0.5, then divide 10 lb by that advantage. Uncover 8.00 : 1 and 1.25 lb. Select Axe. Length 2.00 in and thickness 1.00 in give 2.00 : 1 and 5.00 lb. The short thick head takes four times the push of the long thin chisel.

08 Shop floor Shop / engineering: wedge (splitting force — length ÷ thickness)

Lengthover thickness.

Use this page next to a chisel, a doorstop, or a splitting wedge. The classroom use is Shop / engineering: wedge (splitting force — length ÷ thickness).

08.1

Learning objectives

  • Say that a wedge is two inclined planes back to back, or one face against a stop.
  • Compute ideal mechanical advantage as slope length ÷ thickness.
  • Find the effort for a 10 lb split: effort = load ÷ advantage.
  • Compare a long thin chisel with a short thick axe head.

08.2

Classroom loop

  1. Cover the readout. Select Chisel. Students compute 4.00 ÷ 0.50 and the effort for a 10 lb split.
  2. Uncover 8.00 : 1 and 1.25 lb.
  3. Select Axe. Students predict 2.00 ÷ 1.00 and 5.00 lb. The push is four times the chisel.
  4. Try Doorstop, then the splitting wedge. Length ÷ thickness still sets the effort. A longer face or a thinner end makes the push lighter.

08.3

On the job

A carpenter drives a doorstop under a door. The same carpenter drives a wood-splitting wedge into a log. A mason or a stoneworker sets a chisel in stone, or sets plug-and-feathers in a line of holes. A millwright or a mechanic slides a flat bar into a gap. The thin end of the bar works as a wedge.

The split force stays the same. Length and thickness change how hard you push. A long thin wedge has a higher mechanical advantage, so the push is lighter and the gap opens slowly. A short thick wedge has a lower mechanical advantage, so the push is harder and the gap opens more for each inch you drive. A chisel eases into wood. An axe head needs a heavy blow.
