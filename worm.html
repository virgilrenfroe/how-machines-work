13 Bench Shop / engineering: worm gear (big reduction at 90°)

Count the starts.

Fine is one start and 40 teeth, about 40 : 1. Coarse is two starts and 20 teeth, 10 : 1. Demo is one start and 20 teeth, so one turn of the worm moves the wheel by one tooth.

What a worm is for

Slow, then the hold

The wheel has 40 teeth and the worm has 1 start. Speed ratio N wheel / starts is 40 / 1 = 40 : 1.

The worm shaft and the wheel shaft cross at 90°. They do not meet.

The worm is a helical incline, the same idea as a screw, wrapped around a shaft. The wheel tooth rides that incline.

Hold is on. Try the wheel and the worm does not turn. This switch is a simplified picture. A real worm holds only when friction on the thread is high enough.

Try this

Select Fine. Cover the readout and divide 40 by 1. Uncover 40 : 1. The worm turns and the wheel creeps. The shafts stay crossed at 90°. Choose Try the wheel while Hold is on. The pair stops. The wheel does not turn the worm. Switch to Free reverse and try the wheel again. The motion runs backward. That switch is simplified: a real worm holds only when friction is high enough, and this bench does not calculate friction. Select Coarse and compute 20 / 2 before you look. The thread is steeper and the ratio is 10 : 1. Select Demo. One turn of the worm moves the wheel by one tooth.

13 Shop floor Shop / engineering: worm gear (big reduction at 90°)

Bigand slow.

Use this page next to a worm hoist, a reducer on a conveyor, or the dial on a dividing head. The classroom use is Shop / engineering: worm gear (big reduction at 90°).

13.1

Learning objectives

  • Compute speed ratio as N wheel / starts. A one-start worm is about N wheel : 1.
  • State that the shafts cross at 90° and do not meet.
  • Contrast a worm with a bevel pair: both can turn 90°, but a bevel ratio is tooth count over tooth count and the shafts meet.
  • Explain that a worm is a helical incline, like a screw, and that a hold happens only when friction is high enough.

13.2

Classroom loop

  1. Cover the readout. Set Fine. Students divide 40 teeth by 1 start.
  2. Uncover 40 : 1 and the 90° cross. Watch the wheel creep while the worm turns.
  3. Choose Try the wheel with Hold on. The pair stops. Students say the wheel does not drive the worm.
  4. Switch to Free reverse and try the wheel again. Motion runs backward. Students say the switch is simplified and a real hold depends on friction.
  5. Set Coarse. Students compute 20 / 2 before looking. Set Demo and count one tooth per turn of the worm.

13.3

On the job

A millwright on a worm-gear hoist or winch turns the worm to lift the load. The wheel has many teeth and the worm often has one start, so the handwheel or the motor turns many times while the drum creeps. When the person lets go, a single-start worm often holds the load, because the wheel cannot walk back down the thread if friction is high enough. Many hoists still add a brake. The hold on this bench is a simplified picture of that idea.

An elevator or conveyor technician finds a worm reducer between a fast motor and a slow drum or belt. The shafts cross at 90°, so the motor can sit beside the machine instead of in line with it. The speed ratio is still the wheel teeth divided by the starts. A 40-tooth wheel and a one-start worm is about 40 : 1. The technician counts teeth and starts when a reducer is replaced, because the wrong pair changes the speed even though the shafts still cross at a right angle.

A tuning-machine or indexing-table technician uses a worm when a dial or a table must take a small step and then stay. One turn of the worm advances the wheel by one tooth when the worm has one start. The handwheel sits on the side of the machine, because the shafts cross at 90°. If friction is high enough, the table does not drift backward after the step.

When you need a big slowdown and often a hold, use a worm.
